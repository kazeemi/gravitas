================================================================================
EXECUTIVE PRESENCE COACHING PLATFORM
SCORING METHODOLOGY v4.0
================================================================================
This document is the single source of truth for all scoring logic, dimension
definitions, signal sources, coaching feedback standards, composite
calculation, and gating rules. Every decision the scoring engine makes must
trace back to this document. Any ambiguity is resolved by returning to these
definitions — never by inference or assumption.

================================================================================
SECTION 1 — CRITICAL IMPLEMENTATION RULES
================================================================================

1.1 SIGNAL SOURCES — NON-NEGOTIABLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every dimension is scored from one designated source only. Using a different
source — even as a supplement — is a scoring error.

SOURCE A: gpt-audio acoustic analysis
  Used for: Articulation, Projection, Vocal Tone, Vocal Steadiness,
            Intonation, Breath Control, Confidence Language (vocal
            delivery of phrases), Filler Words (sub-signal)

SOURCE B: Audio waveform numeric signals
  Used for: Pace (WPM overall, WPM per window, rush events),
            Pausing (pause count, duration, placement),
            Intonation (F0 min, F0 max, F0 SD),
            Projection (RMS amplitude mean and SD),
            Vocal Steadiness (F0 SD, RMS SD)

SOURCE C: gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
  Used for: Structure, Conciseness, Confidence Language (phrase
            identification), Context classification (prompt text only —
            never the session transcript)

SOURCE D: Claude Vision video frame analysis
  Used for: Eye Contact, Facial Expression, Gestures, Posture

THE TRANSCRIPT MUST NEVER BE USED FOR:
  Articulation, Projection, Vocal Tone, Vocal Steadiness, Intonation,
  Pace, Pausing, Breath Control. These are audio signal dimensions.
  Reading the transcript to infer how the speaker sounded is a
  scoring error that will produce inconsistent results.

VIDEO FRAMES MUST NEVER BE USED FOR:
  Any audio dimension. Observing that a speaker looks nervous does
  not score Vocal Steadiness. Observing hand movements does not
  score Breath Control.

FILLER WORD CAVEAT:
  Filler words are detected by gpt-audio from the acoustic signal.
  This is reliable for clearly-spoken fillers. Soft or swallowed
  filler sounds may occasionally be missed. The count is a reliable
  estimate, not an exact integer. Score anchors are written in ranges
  to account for this margin. The transcript-derived regex count is
  calculated by the system but must be discarded. The gpt-audio count
  is authoritative.

1.2 FEEDBACK STANDARDS — ALL FOUR MUST BE MET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every dimension coaching output must meet all four standards.
If any standard is not met, the feedback must be regenerated.

STANDARD 1 — EVIDENCE BASED:
  Reference a specific measured value from this session.
  Never use generic observations.
  WRONG: "You spoke too fast."
  RIGHT: "Your pace averaged 182 words per minute. For a difficult
  conversation context, the ideal range is 110–135 words per minute."

STANDARD 2 — IMPACT FRAMED:
  For scores 1–6: state what is happening to the audience right now
  as a result of this signal.
  "At 182 words per minute in a sensitive conversation, your audience
  is processing your speed rather than your message. The gravity this
  moment calls for is not coming through in your delivery."

  For scores 7–10: state what would change if the speaker develops
  this further.
  "You are already controlling your pace well at 138 words per minute.
  If you add deliberate deceleration on every key recommendation —
  not just the headline — your audience will feel the weight of each
  point individually, not just the main one."

STANDARD 3 — COACHING TONE:
  End with one specific, doable next step. Not a list. One action.
  Sized for a single practice session. Encouraging without being
  hollow — no positive statement without evidence to support it.
  WRONG: "Great work on your pace — keep it up!"
  RIGHT: "Your pace control is a genuine strength at 138 words per
  minute. Before your next session, record 90 seconds on a complex
  topic and consciously slow below 120 words per minute on every
  sentence containing a recommendation. Listen back and notice how
  the weight of each point changes."

STANDARD 4 — PLAIN LANGUAGE:
  No technical terms visible to the user.

  F0 / fundamental frequency  →  pitch / how your voice rises and falls
  Hz                          →  describe the effect, omit the unit
  RMS amplitude               →  volume / how your voice carries
  dB                          →  describe the effect, omit the unit
  Standard deviation / SD     →  consistency / variation / steadiness
  WPM                         →  words per minute (spell out first use)
  Filler words per minute     →  how often filler words appear
  ASR confidence              →  clarity / how clearly each word landed
  Mid-clause pause            →  pausing mid-thought
  Boundary pause              →  pausing between ideas
  F0 SD                       →  pitch consistency
  RMS SD                      →  volume consistency
  gpt-audio / Claude Vision   →  never mentioned in user-facing text

SECTION 2 — SESSION REQUIREMENTS
================================================================================

MINIMUM ACTIVE SPEECH: 60 seconds (silence excluded from calculation)
MAXIMUM SESSION LENGTH: 10 minutes
BELOW MINIMUM: Return "Insufficient data — please record at least 60
  seconds of speech." No dimension scores. No composite. No feedback.

AUDIO QUALITY FLAG:
  If overall ASR confidence falls below 70%, attach to all audio results:
  "Audio quality was lower than ideal. Scores may be less precise.
  Try recording in a quieter environment with your microphone closer."
  Score with available data. Do not withhold scores.

VIDEO QUALITY THRESHOLD:
  Face must be detectable for at least 80% of recording duration.
  Below 80%: switch to audio-only mode automatically.
  Notify user: "Your face was not clearly visible for enough of this
  recording to score your Physical Delivery dimensions. These have been
  marked as unavailable. Ensure your face is fully in frame throughout
  your next session."
  Do not attempt to score video dimensions on insufficient frame data.

================================================================================
SECTION 3 — CONTEXT CLASSIFICATION
================================================================================

Before scoring any dimension, the AI reads the prompt text and classifies
the session into one of five communication context categories. This
classification determines the ideal WPM range for the Pace dimension and
is referenced explicitly in coaching feedback so the user understands
which standard was applied to their session.

Classification uses the prompt text only — never the session transcript.

If classification is ambiguous: default to Category 5 (Conversational).
State in feedback: "This prompt was classified as conversational. If you
were practising a different context, resubmit with a more specific prompt
and the scoring standard will adjust accordingly."

CATEGORY 1: HIGH ENERGY / VISION / INSPIRATION
  Examples: vision statements, motivational address, rallying a team,
            announcing a bold strategic direction, inspiring action
  Ideal WPM: 145–175

CATEGORY 2: FORMAL PRESENTATION / SENIOR STAKEHOLDER
  Examples: board presentation, investor pitch, executive update,
            C-suite briefing, formal report-out to leadership
  Ideal WPM: 120–145

CATEGORY 3: ANALYTICAL / TECHNICAL / COMPLEX
  Examples: technical explanation, data walkthrough, strategic analysis,
            detailed recommendation with supporting rationale,
            complex problem framing
  Ideal WPM: 115–145

CATEGORY 4: DIFFICULT CONVERSATION / SENSITIVE
  Examples: delivering feedback, managing conflict, sharing difficult
            news, sensitive personnel conversations, apologies,
            addressing underperformance
  Ideal WPM: 110–135

CATEGORY 5: CONVERSATIONAL / IMPROMPTU / INTERVIEW
  Examples: everyday professional exchange, impromptu questions,
            interview answers, corridor conversations, informal updates,
            competency questions
  Ideal WPM: 130–160

SECTION 4 — SCORING TIERS
================================================================================

  Score 1–3   |  Needs Work      — Signals are absent, severely deficient,
               |                    or actively undermining presence. The
               |                    audience is distracted, confused, or
               |                    unconvinced by the delivery itself,
               |                    independent of the content.

  Score 4–6   |  Developing      — Foundation is present but inconsistently
               |                    applied. The skill exists but has not
               |                    become habitual or reliable under pressure.

  Score 7–8   |  Strong          — Consistent, controlled, credible delivery.
               |                    The dimension is working in the speaker's
               |                    favour the majority of the time. This is
               |                    a genuinely good score. A 7 is solid
               |                    professional standard.

  Score 9–10  |  Distinguished   — Masterful and intentional. The listener
               |                    notices it positively. Score 9 means the
               |                    AI found almost nothing to improve.
               |                    Score 10 is reserved for world-class
               |                    delivery — exceptionally rare, awarded
               |                    only when no meaningful gap exists.

SECTION 5 — THE FIFTEEN DIMENSIONS
================================================================================

PILLAR 1: VOICE QUALITY (Dimensions 1–4) — Audio source
  The raw instrument. The intrinsic qualities of the voice that determine
  whether it commands attention and is easy to listen to, independent of
  what is said or how it is delivered.

PILLAR 2: VOCAL DELIVERY (Dimensions 5–8) — Audio source
  How the voice is used. The deliberate control of speed, rhythm,
  emphasis, silence, and breath to guide the listener and make the
  message land with maximum impact.

PILLAR 3: THOUGHT CLARITY (Dimensions 9–11) — Transcript source
  How thinking is organised and expressed. Whether communication is
  confident, structured, and precise, or meanders, hedges, and leaves
  the listener doing the work.

PILLAR 4: PHYSICAL DELIVERY (Dimensions 12–15) — Video source
  What the audience sees. The visual signals that confirm or contradict
  everything the voice is saying. Scored in video sessions only.

DIMENSION 1 — ARTICULATION
Pillar: Voice Quality | Weight: 4% (audio-only: 5%) | Source: gpt-audio
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
How clearly and precisely words are formed in the audio signal. Whether
every word lands fully — including consonants, vowels, and word endings —
or whether sounds are dropped, slurred, or muffled. Scored from audio
analysis only. Never from reading the transcript.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-audio word-level clarity assessment
  - Existing articulation dimension score from audio engine as reference

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Audio reveals almost incomprehensible delivery. Heavy mumbling, severely
dropped word endings, slurred speech throughout. A listener cannot follow
without replaying. Articulation is the dominant obstacle in the recording.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Multiple words per sentence are unclear. Dropped endings are constant.
Comprehension requires significant effort. The speaker's meaning is
frequently lost at the word level.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Intermittent but frequent mumbling audible in the signal. Word endings
dropped regularly. At least one unclear passage per minute. Comprehension
is possible but the listener must actively compensate.

Score 4 — Developing:
Articulation problems appear regularly, particularly at higher pace or
under pressure. The listener catches most words but must fill gaps.
Word endings inconsistently present — some sentences clear, others not.

Score 5 — Developing:
Occasional mumbling or dropped endings, typically in faster passages.
Generally comprehensible but a listener would remark on slippage if asked.
One or two unclear words per minute on average.

Score 6 — Developing:
Minor articulation lapses only. Listener can follow without sustained
effort. Occasional softening at sentence ends in faster passages but
these are exceptions, not the pattern.

Score 7 — Strong:
All words land clearly. Only very subtle, infrequent softening at sentence
ends that does not impede comprehension. The listener never has to work
to follow the speaker.

Score 8 — Strong:
Crisp, precise articulation throughout. Every word is deliberate and
fully formed. Consonants clean. Vowels open. Word endings consistent.
Articulation is an asset, not merely neutral.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional diction. Each consonant and vowel is intentional. No
drop-off at speed or volume changes. The voice is a precision instrument
at every moment of the recording.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
Broadcast-standard enunciation. No listener would identify a single
unclear word at any point. A benchmark for other speakers.
Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Identify specific passages where clarity breaks down.
  Impact: "When words are unclear, your audience shifts attention from
  your message to decoding your speech. You lose them at the word level
  before they can engage with your ideas."
Scores 4–6: Name the specific pattern — dropped endings, mumbling under
  pressure, or speed-related slippage. "Your articulation is generally
  solid but drops noticeably when your pace increases. Your audience
  notices the inconsistency even if they cannot name it."
Scores 7–8: Confirm what is working. Identify the one remaining
  opportunity. "Your articulation is clear and consistent. The next
  level is maintaining the same precision when your energy rises."
Scores 9–10: Name what makes it distinctive and its impact on the
  audience's experience of listening to this speaker.

DIMENSION 2 — PROJECTION
Pillar: Voice Quality | Weight: 6% (audio-only: 7%)
Source: gpt-audio + RMS amplitude signals
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether the voice carries with consistent authority from the beginning
to the end of each phrase. Projection is not simply being loud — it is
the sustained energy that keeps the voice audible, present, and
commanding throughout every sentence. Scored from audio waveform
analysis of RMS amplitude values.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Average RMS amplitude in dB across the session
  - RMS amplitude standard deviation across the session (volume consistency)
  - Phrase-ending amplitude analysis (does volume drop at phrase endings?)
  - gpt-audio volume assessment as qualitative corroboration

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:

  RMS amplitude SD:
    Needs Work:    SD > 12 dB — severe inconsistency, frequent drops
    Developing:    SD 8–12 dB — moderate inconsistency
    Strong:        SD 4–8 dB — controlled with intentional variation
    Distinguished: SD < 4 dB with deliberate dynamic range use

  Phrase-ending amplitude drop:
    Needs Work:    > 50% amplitude drop at phrase endings consistently
    Developing:    30–50% drop at phrase endings frequently
    Strong:        < 20% drop — phrases well-supported throughout
    Distinguished: < 10% drop — phrases fully supported to the last word

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Audio waveform shows severely insufficient or erratic amplitude. Either
inaudible passages are frequent, or volume spikes create discomfort.
Comprehension is impossible in multiple sections. RMS SD > 14 dB.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Severely under-projected. Multiple phrases fade to near-inaudibility.
Phrase-ending drops exceed 50% consistently. RMS SD > 12 dB. The
listener strains throughout.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Frequently too quiet — trailing off in at least 30% of sentences.
Projection drops under pressure or in complex passages. RMS SD 10–12 dB.
Listener must compensate for consistent volume loss.

Score 4 — Developing:
Volume a noticeable issue. Often trails at sentence endings. Projection
drops when content becomes complex or emotional. RMS SD 8–10 dB.
Phrase-ending drops of 30–50% appear regularly.

Score 5 — Developing:
Mostly audible but with recurring dip at sentence endings or in longer
phrases. Adequate but not commanding. RMS SD 8–9 dB. Listener can
follow but projection does not sustain authority throughout.

Score 6 — Developing:
Consistently audible. Projection holds through sentences with minor
drops. RMS SD 6–8 dB. Phrase endings mostly supported. Functional
but no deliberate dynamic range in evidence.

Score 7 — Strong:
Good projection throughout. Volume controlled and sustaining authority.
RMS SD 4–6 dB. Very occasional softening only. The voice carries
without the listener needing to compensate at any point.

Score 8 — Strong:
Strong, confident projection. Volume used as an active tool — speaker
quiets intentionally for emphasis, then projects fully. RMS SD 4–6 dB
with evidence of deliberate dynamic range. No unplanned drops.
Phrase endings fully supported.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional command of volume. Every word carries. RMS SD < 4 dB with
controlled, purposeful dynamic range use. Phrase-ending drop < 10%.
The speaker's voice fills the space without strain at every moment.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
Outstanding projection and dynamic control. Every phrase is fully
supported. Volume modulation is sophisticated and deliberate throughout.
A model of vocal authority. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Reference specific RMS values and phrase-ending drop pattern.
  Impact: "When your voice drops at the end of sentences, you lose the
  authority of the point you just made. Your audience hears the
  uncertainty in the fade, even if your words were confident."
Scores 4–6: Identify the specific pattern — trailing at phrase endings,
  dropping under pressure, or overall low amplitude.
  Impact: "A voice that doesn't carry its full authority throughout
  each sentence signals effort to the listener. They work to hear you
  rather than simply receiving what you're saying."
Scores 7–8: Confirm the strength. Identify the one opportunity.
Scores 9–10: Name what makes it exceptional and its impact.

DIMENSION 3 — VOCAL TONE
Pillar: Voice Quality | Weight: 8% (audio-only: 9%) | Source: gpt-audio
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
The richness, warmth, and resonance of the voice as an instrument. Vocal
tone is what makes a voice compelling to listen to independently of what
it is saying or how fast it speaks. A voice with strong tone commands
attention and conveys credibility before the words are processed. Thin,
nasal, strained, or breathy tone undermines authority regardless of
content or delivery skill. Scored from gpt-audio acoustic analysis only.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-audio tonal quality assessment
  - Presence of breathiness, nasality, strain, or resonance in the signal
  - Consistency of tonal quality across the session

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Tone is severely problematic — heavily strained, extremely breathy,
or so thin that authority is absent entirely. The quality of the voice
itself creates negative perception before any content is processed.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Very thin, nasal, or strained throughout. Significant breathiness
or pressed quality that makes the voice uncomfortable to listen to
over a sustained period. Tonal quality is a primary obstacle.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Consistently thin or breathy tone without moments of resonance.
The voice lacks the warmth and weight that commands attention.
Listeners notice the tonal deficiency even if they cannot name it.

Score 4 — Developing:
Tone is adequate but unremarkable. Occasional moments of resonance
undermined by frequent thinning, breathiness, or nasal quality.
The voice does not work against the speaker but does not work for them.

Score 5 — Developing:
Tone is generally acceptable with recurring thinning under pressure
or in sustained passages. Some warmth present but inconsistent.
The voice carries the message without enhancing it.

Score 6 — Developing:
Solid tonal quality for most of the session. Minor inconsistencies
in resonance. The voice is pleasant to listen to without being
distinctly compelling. No significant tonal obstacles.

Score 7 — Strong:
Good, warm tonal quality that supports authority and engagement.
Resonance is consistent. Only very minor inconsistencies. The voice
is an asset to the speaker's credibility.

Score 8 — Strong:
Rich, resonant tone throughout. The voice conveys warmth, authority,
and confidence acoustically before the words are processed. Listeners
would describe the voice as compelling. Consistent across the session.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional vocal resonance. The voice commands attention
independently of content or delivery. Warmth and authority are
both present simultaneously throughout. A voice that is genuinely
pleasurable and authoritative to listen to.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class vocal tone. The voice itself is a primary presence signal.
Rich, resonant, warm, and authoritative in every moment of the
recording. Exceptionally rare — a voice that would be remarked upon
by any listener.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Name the specific tonal quality observed — thin, strained,
  breathy, or nasal. Impact: "Tone quality is the first thing your
  audience registers before your words land. A thin or strained voice
  signals effort or anxiety, which creates doubt about your confidence
  before you have made a single point."
Scores 4–6: "Your voice carries the message but does not yet amplify
  it. Developing more resonance would make your words land with more
  weight without changing a single thing you say."
Scores 7–10: Confirm strength and specific quality. Higher scores:
  "Your vocal tone is a genuine presence asset — it creates authority
  before your content does."

DIMENSION 4 — VOCAL STEADINESS
Pillar: Voice Quality | Weight: 2% (audio-only: 3%)
Source: gpt-audio + F0 SD + RMS SD signals
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
The absence of anxiety signals in the voice — audible tremor, irregular
pitch fluctuation, or tension-driven strain that signals the nervous
system under pressure. A steady voice signals composure. Unsteadiness
signals anxiety regardless of what the words say. This dimension measures
the observable audio signal only — it does not attempt to diagnose inner
state.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - F0 standard deviation across the session (pitch consistency)
  - RMS amplitude standard deviation (volume consistency)
  - gpt-audio assessment of audible tremor and vocal tension
  - Note: Jitter (cycle-to-cycle F0 variation) is not currently available
    from the audio engine. F0 SD serves as the available proxy.

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:

  F0 SD as proxy for pitch stability:
    Needs Work:    F0 SD > 35 Hz combined with audible tremor
    Developing:    F0 SD 20–35 Hz with occasional unsteadiness
    Strong:        F0 SD < 20 Hz, voice steady throughout
    Distinguished: F0 SD < 12 Hz, voice is a model of composure

  Note: F0 SD reflects both intentional pitch variation (Intonation,
  Dimension 5) and uncontrolled fluctuation (Vocal Steadiness). When
  F0 SD is high, cross-reference with gpt-audio assessment to determine
  whether variation is expressive (scores well in Intonation) or
  anxious (scores poorly in Vocal Steadiness). These are distinct signals
  scored in different dimensions.

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Audible tremor is continuous throughout the recording. The voice
shakes in a way the listener cannot ignore. Pitch fluctuation is
erratic and clearly anxiety-driven. The speaker sounds distressed.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Frequent audible tremor throughout. Multiple passages where the
voice shakes noticeably. Significant uncontrolled pitch fluctuation.
Anxiety signal is dominant and persistent.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Intermittent but recurring audible tremor — noticeable in at least
30% of the recording. Unsteadiness appears consistently under any
moment of pressure or complexity.

Score 4 — Developing:
Occasional unsteadiness audible in the signal. Tremor or pitch
instability appears in higher-pressure passages. Generally controlled
but with enough instances to be noticeable to the listener.

Score 5 — Developing:
Mostly steady with one or two moments of audible unsteadiness.
The voice is generally under control but the listener would notice
the lapses if asked to listen specifically for them.

Score 6 — Developing:
Generally steady throughout with very occasional minor wavering that
does not create a pattern. The voice conveys reasonable composure.
Minor instability only at peak pressure moments.

Score 7 — Strong:
Steady and controlled throughout the recording. No audible tremor.
Pitch is consistent and deliberate. The voice conveys composure.
F0 SD is in the controlled range for the content type.

Score 8 — Strong:
Noticeably steady. The voice is an active signal of composure —
not just the absence of anxiety but the presence of settledness.
F0 SD low and consistent. No moments of wavering under pressure.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional steadiness. The voice sounds equally settled at the
beginning, middle, and end — and equally at moments of low and
high stakes content. F0 SD < 12 Hz. A listener would describe
this speaker as completely composed.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class vocal composure. The voice is a precision signal of
total settledness. No moment of unsteadiness across the entire
recording. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Name the specific pattern — continuous tremor, pressure-
  triggered wavering, or general unsteadiness.
  Impact: "When the voice trembles or wavers, your audience reads it
  as anxiety. Even if your words are confident, the physical signal
  in your voice is telling them a different story. They hear both."
Scores 4–6: "Your voice is generally steady but shows unsteadiness
  under pressure. Your audience notices this even briefly — it creates
  a moment of doubt about your composure precisely when composure
  matters most."
Scores 7–10: Confirm what composure signal is present and its effect.
  "A steady voice is one of the most powerful presence signals
  available to you — it tells the room you are settled before you
  have made a single argument."

DIMENSION 5 — INTONATION
Pillar: Vocal Delivery | Weight: 8% (audio-only: 10%)
Source: gpt-audio + F0 signals (min, max, SD)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
The variation in pitch across the delivery — whether the voice rises
and falls purposefully to signal emphasis, structure, and meaning, or
whether it stays flat and monotone regardless of content. Intonation
is how spoken words feel different from each other. A voice with
strong intonation guides the listener through the content; a flat
voice forces the listener to extract meaning without acoustic signposting.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - F0 minimum (Hz) — lowest pitch in voiced segments
  - F0 maximum (Hz) — highest pitch in voiced segments
  - F0 range (F0 max minus F0 min) — total pitch span available
  - F0 standard deviation — how actively the pitch range is used
  - Pitch variation ordinal score (1–5 from audio engine)
  - gpt-audio intonation quality assessment

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:

  F0 range (max minus min):
    Needs Work:    < 30 Hz (effectively monotone)
    Developing:    30–60 Hz (limited variation)
    Strong:        60–100 Hz (expressive range in use)
    Distinguished: > 100 Hz (full range, deliberately deployed)

  F0 SD (how actively the range is used):
    Needs Work:    < 8 Hz SD (flat delivery within narrow range)
    Developing:    8–18 Hz SD (some variation, not purposeful)
    Strong:        18–30 Hz SD (deliberate, consistent variation)
    Distinguished: > 30 Hz SD with purposeful patterns detectable

  Pitch variation ordinal (from audio engine):
    1 = monotone, 2 = minimal, 3 = moderate, 4 = expressive,
    5 = highly expressive

  Cross-reference rule: F0 SD is shared with Vocal Steadiness
  (Dimension 4). High F0 SD that is accompanied by gpt-audio
  assessment of expressive intonation scores well here. High F0 SD
  accompanied by gpt-audio assessment of tremor or anxiety scores
  poorly in Vocal Steadiness instead. These are mutually exclusive
  interpretations of the same numeric signal.

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
F0 range < 20 Hz. Pitch variation score 1. Delivery is completely
monotone. Every sentence sounds acoustically identical regardless of
content. The listener disengages. No emphasis is audible anywhere
in the recording.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
F0 range 20–30 Hz. Pitch variation score 1–2. Effectively monotone.
Very rare pitch movement that creates no meaningful pattern. The voice
provides no acoustic guidance through the content.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
F0 range 25–35 Hz. Pitch variation score 2. Occasional pitch variation
but no purposeful pattern. Mostly flat with isolated moments of
movement that do not signal emphasis or structure.

Score 4 — Developing:
F0 range 30–50 Hz. Pitch variation score 2–3. Some variation present
but inconsistent and unpatterned. Moments of expressiveness undercut
by extended flat passages. The listener gets occasional acoustic
guidance but not reliably.

Score 5 — Developing:
F0 range 40–60 Hz. Pitch variation score 3. Moderate variation with
a recurring problem — either the range is adequate but not deployed
purposefully, or it is deployed in some sections and absent in others.

Score 6 — Developing:
F0 range 50–70 Hz. Pitch variation score 3. Solid variation with
intonation generally supporting the content. Minor inconsistencies —
some passages more expressive than others. The listener is guided
most but not all of the time.

Score 7 — Strong:
F0 range 60–90 Hz. Pitch variation score 3–4. F0 SD 18–25 Hz.
Deliberate intonation that consistently signals emphasis and structure.
The voice rises and falls purposefully. The listener is guided
through the content by the acoustic signal reliably.

Score 8 — Strong:
F0 range 75–100 Hz. Pitch variation score 4. F0 SD 22–30 Hz.
Expressive intonation clearly deployed as a communication tool.
Key points marked by audible pitch movement. The voice is dynamic
and sustains engagement throughout.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
F0 range > 100 Hz. Pitch variation score 4–5. F0 SD > 28 Hz.
Full pitch range in deliberate use. Intonation patterns are
sophisticated — the voice modulates to serve the structure of the
argument, not just individual words. A listener could follow the
structure of the argument from pitch alone.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
F0 range > 120 Hz. Pitch variation score 5. F0 SD consistently high
and purposeful. Intonation is a precision instrument. Every pitch
movement is intentional and serves the communication. World-class
vocal expressiveness. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: State F0 range and pitch variation score.
  Impact: "A flat voice makes every sentence sound equally important,
  which means nothing feels important. Your audience hears a single
  tone throughout and their attention drifts — not because your
  content is weak, but because your voice is not guiding them to
  what matters."
Scores 4–6: Name the specific gap — limited range, undeployed range,
  or inconsistency. "You have pitch variation available but it is not
  yet working consistently as a tool. The moments where your voice
  becomes expressive show what is possible. The next step is making
  that the pattern, not the exception."
Scores 7–8: Confirm the strength and identify the next level.
Scores 9–10: Name the specific patterns that made it distinguished.

DIMENSION 6 — PACE
Pillar: Vocal Delivery | Weight: 7% (audio-only: 8%)
Source: Audio waveform — WPM signals
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether speaking speed is controlled, appropriate for the context, and
purposefully varied. Pace is not simply about being fast or slow — it is
about whether speed serves the message at every moment. The same WPM that
signals energy in a vision statement signals carelessness in a difficult
conversation. This dimension scores both the appropriateness of overall
pace and the intentionality of how it changes across the session.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - WPM overall (total words divided by total active speech seconds)
  - WPM per 30-second window (array of values)
  - WPM standard deviation across windows (pace consistency)
  - Rush events (bursts exceeding 200 WPM for 5 or more consecutive words)
  - Deceleration events (windows where WPM drops below 80% of session
    average — potential signal of intentional emphasis)
  - Context classification (determines ideal WPM range)

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE BY CONTEXT:

  Context 1 — High Energy / Vision:          Ideal WPM 145–175
  Context 2 — Formal / Senior Stakeholder:   Ideal WPM 120–145
  Context 3 — Analytical / Technical:        Ideal WPM 115–145
  Context 4 — Difficult Conversation:        Ideal WPM 110–135
  Context 5 — Conversational / Interview:    Ideal WPM 130–160

  "Within range" = within the ideal WPM band for the classified context.
  "Outside range" = above or below the ideal band.

  Rush events:
    Needs Work:    4 or more rush events
    Developing:    2–3 rush events
    Strong:        0–1 rush events
    Distinguished: 0 rush events

  WPM SD across 30-second windows:
    Needs Work:    SD > 40 (uncontrolled variance)
    Developing:    SD 26–40
    Strong:        SD 11–25
    Distinguished: SD < 11 with deliberate deceleration pattern detectable

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
WPM is more than 50 above or below the context ideal. Pace makes
content incomprehensible or unlistenable. 4 or more rush events.
WPM SD > 45. The listener cannot follow. Pace is the primary obstacle.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
WPM is 35–50 outside the context ideal. Rush events are frequent
(3–4). SD > 40. Delivery consistently difficult to follow. No
evidence of intentional pace variation anywhere in the session.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
WPM is 20–35 outside the context ideal, or within range but with
3+ rush events and SD > 35. Pace is a recurring distraction. The
listener notices the speed problem multiple times.

Score 4 — Developing:
WPM is 15–20 outside the context ideal, or within range but with
2–3 rush events. SD 30–40. Some sections work; others rush or drag
noticeably. The listener is compensating for the pace problem.

Score 5 — Developing:
WPM generally close to context ideal (within 15 WPM) but with a
recurring pattern — typically speeding up under pressure or in
longer passages. 2 rush events. SD 26–35. The listener can follow
but occasionally feels pace working against the content.

Score 6 — Developing:
WPM within context ideal range for the majority of the session.
1–2 rush events. SD 20–30. Functional and rarely distracting.
However no evidence of deliberate pace variation as a tool.

Score 7 — Strong:
WPM consistently within context ideal. 0–1 rush events. SD 15–25.
The pace serves the content — the listener can follow without effort.
At least one detectable deceleration on a key point. Minor missed
opportunities for further variation.

Score 8 — Strong:
WPM well-controlled within context ideal. 0 rush events. SD 11–20.
Clear evidence of intentional pace modulation — speaker demonstrably
slows on recommendations or key statements, moves faster through
context and background. Pace is an active tool, not merely controlled.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
WPM precisely controlled within context ideal. 0 rush events.
SD < 11. Deliberate deceleration detectable across multiple windows
tied to content importance — a consistent pattern, not isolated
instances. A listener would describe the delivery as "controlled"
without being prompted.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
All criteria for 9. Tempo variation is sophisticated and sustained
throughout — every significant moment marked by a detectable shift
in pace. Speed serves structure at every point. Exceptionally rare.

EDGE CASE RULES:
  - WPM within ideal range but 3+ rush events: cap score at 5.
    Control has broken down under pressure regardless of average.
  - WPM outside ideal range but 0 rush events and SD < 20: floor at 5.
    Pace is off but the control is genuine.
  - Sessions under 90 seconds: fewer than 3 windows available.
    Note this in output. Weight overall WPM and rush events more
    heavily than SD for short sessions.
  - Classification ambiguous: default to Category 5. Flag in feedback.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
State actual WPM, the context classification applied, and ideal range.
Scores 1–3: "This prompt was classified as [context], where an ideal
  pace is [range] words per minute. Your average was [X] words per
  minute — [significantly faster/slower] than this context calls for.
  [Number] moments of very fast speech were detected in the second
  half of the session. At this speed, [the gravity / the clarity /
  the authority] the moment requires was not coming through."
Scores 4–6: "Your average pace of [X] words per minute is [within /
  close to] range for this context, but you accelerated above [Y] words
  per minute [number] times — both when moving into your main argument.
  Slowing into your key points rather than speeding up would
  significantly increase their impact on your audience."
Scores 7–8: "Your pace was well-controlled at [X] words per minute
  for a [context] context. You slowed noticeably when delivering your
  key point — that was effective. The next level is making that pattern
  consistent across every significant moment, not just the headline."
Scores 9–10: "Your pace moved between [X] and [Y] words per minute
  in a clear pattern — faster through context, slower into every
  significant point. That is exactly how pace should serve structure."

DIMENSION 7 — PAUSING
Pillar: Vocal Delivery | Weight: 6% (audio-only: 7%)
Source: Audio waveform — pause detection signals
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
The deliberate use of silence as a communication tool. Strategic pauses
at idea boundaries let points land, signal composure, and create emphasis.
The absence of pausing produces rushed, breathless delivery. Pausing
before a key statement — rather than after — is one of the most powerful
signals of executive presence. This dimension is entirely separate from
Pace (which measures speed) and from Vocal Steadiness (which covers
hesitation mid-thought). Only pauses at idea boundaries count here.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Pause count: number of silences of 0.5 seconds or longer
  - Average pause duration in seconds
  - Pause placement: boundary (between ideas) vs mid-clause (hesitation)
  - Pre-statement pause count: pauses occurring before a key statement
    (boundary pause immediately before a declarative or recommendation)
  - Speech duration (to calculate pause frequency per minute)

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:

  Strategic pause frequency (boundary pauses per minute):
    Needs Work:    < 0.5 per minute (effectively no pausing)
    Developing:    0.5–1.5 per minute (occasional, not deliberate)
    Strong:        1.5–3.0 per minute (regular and purposeful)
    Distinguished: 1.5–3.0 per minute with pre-statement pauses present

  Average pause duration:
    Too short:     < 0.4 seconds (micro-gaps, not strategic pauses)
    Functional:    0.5–2.0 seconds (effective emphasis pauses)
    Too long:      > 3.0 seconds (dead air, signals loss of thread)

  Pause placement ratio (boundary vs mid-clause):
    Needs Work:    Majority of pauses are mid-clause
    Developing:    Mixed — boundary and mid-clause roughly equal
    Strong:        Majority of pauses are at boundaries
    Distinguished: Almost all pauses at boundaries with pre-statement
                   pauses present

  Note: Mid-clause pauses are hesitation signals and belong to
  Confidence Language (Dimension 9) as evidence of word-searching.
  They are not counted toward this dimension's strategic pause score.

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Fewer than 0.3 strategic boundary pauses per minute. Speech is
essentially continuous. The listener gets no acoustic breathing room.
Every point runs directly into the next. No strategic use of silence
anywhere in the recording.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
0.3–0.5 boundary pauses per minute, average duration under 0.4
seconds — micro-gaps, not pauses. Or multiple pauses present but
predominantly mid-clause (hesitation, not strategy). No evidence
of silence being used deliberately.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
0.4–0.8 boundary pauses per minute but inconsistently placed and
brief. Occasionally a pause lands well but the pattern is not
established. The listener rarely gets a moment to absorb a point.

Score 4 — Developing:
0.8–1.2 boundary pauses per minute. Some pauses land at idea
boundaries but others are mid-clause or poorly timed. Average
duration functional (0.5–1.0 seconds) but not commanding. The
listener gets occasional space but not reliably.

Score 5 — Developing:
1.0–1.5 boundary pauses per minute at average duration 0.6–1.2
seconds. Generally well-placed but inconsistent — some sections
have good pause structure; others run continuously. No pre-statement
pauses detected.

Score 6 — Developing:
1.2–1.8 boundary pauses per minute. Generally at idea boundaries.
Average duration 0.8–1.5 seconds. Functional and occasionally
effective. The listener gets space to absorb most points. No
pre-statement pauses yet — pausing is happening after points land,
not before.

Score 7 — Strong:
1.5–3.0 boundary pauses per minute. Consistently at idea boundaries.
Average duration 0.8–2.0 seconds. Pausing is a reliable feature of
the delivery. At least 1 pre-statement pause detected — the speaker
settled before at least one key moment before speaking into it.

Score 8 — Strong:
1.5–3.0 boundary pauses per minute. Almost all at boundaries.
Average duration 1.0–2.0 seconds. 2 or more pre-statement pauses
detected. Pausing is clearly an intentional tool. The silences add
weight to what follows them. A listener would notice the use of
silence as purposeful.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
1.5–3.0 boundary pauses per minute with average duration 1.0–2.5
seconds. All pauses at idea boundaries. Multiple pre-statement
pauses present — the speaker consistently settles before key
statements. The pauses feel commanding, not tentative. A listener
would describe the delivery as "deliberate."

Score 10 — Distinguished:
All criteria for 9. The use of silence is sophisticated throughout —
the length of each pause is calibrated to the weight of what
precedes or follows it. Pre-statement pauses are a consistent
pattern, not occasional instances. Silence is as powerful as speech
in this recording. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: State boundary pause frequency and what is happening instead.
  Impact: "Without pauses between ideas, every point you make runs
  directly into the next. Your audience has no moment to absorb what
  you have said before you are already making the next point. The most
  important things you say get lost in the momentum."
Scores 4–6: "You are using pauses occasionally but not yet as a
  deliberate tool. The pauses that land well — [reference timing if
  available] — show what is possible. The next step is making that
  a consistent pattern, particularly before your most important points."
Scores 7–8: "Your use of pauses is a genuine strength. You paused
  before [X] — that gave your audience time to register the weight of
  what followed. The next level is doing that consistently before
  every key recommendation."
Scores 9–10: Name specific pause moments and their impact.
  "The pause before your main recommendation at [timestamp] was
  exactly right — it signalled to the room that what followed mattered."

DIMENSION 8 — BREATH CONTROL
Pillar: Vocal Delivery | Weight: 4% (audio-only: 5%) | Source: gpt-audio
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether breath supports the delivery throughout each phrase. Poor breath
control causes the voice to thin or trail at phrase endings, forces
mid-thought pauses at grammatically wrong moments, and produces audible
inhalations that distract the listener. Strong breath control is the
physical foundation of commanding delivery — it enables full phrases,
sustained projection, and strategic pausing. Scored from gpt-audio
acoustic analysis of the recording.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-audio assessment of phrase support quality (does voice sustain
    through full phrases or thin toward the end?)
  - Audible inhalation count and pattern
  - Breath-induced mid-clause pause count (pauses caused by running
    out of breath rather than by intent — distinct from strategic
    pausing in Dimension 7)
  - Phrase-ending amplitude drop (from Projection signals, used here
    as corroborating evidence of breath insufficiency)

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Breath support is severely inadequate throughout. Multiple phrases
per minute end audibly — the voice runs out before the thought does.
Frequent audible, strained inhalations. Multiple breath-induced
mid-clause pauses per minute. The breathing competes with the message.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Consistent phrase-ending thinning throughout. Audible inhalations
frequent (more than 3 per minute). Breath-induced pauses interrupt
the flow of ideas regularly. The listener is aware of the breathing
as a separate event.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Frequent phrase-ending drops where the voice loses support. Audible
inhalations 2–3 per minute. At least 1 breath-induced mid-clause
pause per minute. Support is inconsistent and the listener notices
the pattern across the session.

Score 4 — Developing:
Phrase-ending thinning present in 30–40% of sentences. Audible
inhalations 1–2 per minute. Occasional breath-induced mid-clause
pause. Generally manageable but support is not reliable under
pressure or in longer phrases.

Score 5 — Developing:
Phrase-ending support mostly adequate but with recurring drops in
longer phrases or higher-pressure passages. Audible inhalations
1 per minute. Breath-induced mid-clause pauses rare but present.

Score 6 — Developing:
Generally well-supported phrases with occasional thinning at
endings in extended sentences. Audible inhalations less than 1
per minute. No breath-induced mid-clause pauses. Functional
breath control without being commanding.

Score 7 — Strong:
Phrases are consistently supported to their endings. Audible
inhalations rare (0–1 in the session). No breath-induced
mid-clause pauses. Breath control does not draw the listener's
attention at any point.

Score 8 — Strong:
Full phrase support throughout. Inhalations inaudible or silent.
Zero breath-induced mid-clause pauses. At least one instance of
a deliberate breath taken before a key statement — using breath
intentionally as a settling signal before important content.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Breathing is entirely in service of delivery — silent, invisible,
and supportive throughout. Full phrase support in every sentence.
Multiple instances of deliberate, visible pre-statement breath
used intentionally to signal composure and weight. A listener
would not register breathing as a separate event.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
All criteria for 9. The relationship between breath and delivery
is sophisticated — breath is used strategically to pace the session
and create deliberate moments of stillness. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Identify the specific pattern — phrase-ending drops,
  audible inhalations, or breath-induced pauses.
  Impact: "When your voice loses support before the end of a sentence,
  the authority of the point disappears with it. Your audience hears
  the trailing as hesitation, even when your words are confident.
  The end of a sentence is where the point lands — it needs to be
  your strongest moment, not your weakest."
Scores 4–6: Name the specific pattern and when it appears.
  "Your breath support is generally solid but drops in longer passages.
  Practise sustaining your voice to the final word of each sentence —
  the audience registers where your voice ends, not just what it said."
Scores 7–8: Confirm the strength. "Try taking one deliberate breath
  before your most important point in your next session. That moment
  of stillness signals to your audience that something important is
  coming."
Scores 9–10: Confirm the mastery and name the specific moments.

DIMENSION 9 — CONFIDENCE LANGUAGE
Pillar: Thought Clarity | Weight: 13% (audio-only: 16%)
Source: gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
The balance between assertive and hedging language as heard and transcribed
in the session. Assertive language — clear statements, direct
recommendations, decisive positions — projects authority. Hedging language
quietly undermines it even when the content is strong. Filler words (um,
uh, like, you know, etc.) are tracked as a diagnostic sub-signal of this
dimension and reported separately in the feedback but feed into this score.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript for phrase identification
  - gpt-audio fillerWords field (authoritative filler count)
  - Assertive language markers: "I know", "we will", "the answer is",
    "my recommendation is", "the data shows", "I am confident",
    clear declarative statements without qualification
  - Hedging language markers: "I think", "maybe", "I guess", "kind of",
    "I believe", "hopefully", "I'm not sure but", "it might be",
    "possibly", "sort of", "I suppose"
  - Filler words (gpt-audio count): um, uh, ah, like (non-prepositional),
    you know, so (as sentence opener with no referent), basically,
    literally (as intensifier), right (tag seeking agreement), okay
    (as filler pause), I mean, kind of, sort of

  Filler rate calculation:
    Filler words per minute = filler count divided by speech duration
    in minutes

  FILLER RATE QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:
    Needs Work:    > 6 fillers per minute
    Developing:    3–6 fillers per minute
    Strong:        1–2 fillers per minute
    Distinguished: 0–1 fillers per minute across the full session

  ASSERTIVE TO HEDGING RATIO:
    Needs Work:    More hedges than assertions (ratio < 1:1)
    Developing:    Roughly equal (ratio 1:1 to 2:1)
    Strong:        Assertive language dominant (ratio 3:1 or higher)
    Distinguished: Highly assertive throughout (ratio 5:1 or higher)
                   with active use of conclusive language

  FILLER CAVEAT FOR SCORING:
    The filler count from gpt-audio is a reliable estimate, not an
    exact integer. Soft or swallowed fillers may occasionally be
    missed. Score anchors use ranges to account for this margin.
    Do not penalise a speaker for being at the boundary of two bands
    if the filler rate is on the cusp — use the assertive/hedge ratio
    and overall impression to resolve ties.

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Language is actively undermining. Nearly every statement hedged or
qualified. Filler rate > 8 per minute. Assertive language absent or
rare. The speaker sounds uncertain of every claim they make.
Hedge ratio < 1:2 (more than twice as many hedges as assertions).

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Pervasive hedging. Filler rate 6–8 per minute. Multiple types of
hedge words dominate throughout. Any assertive language is
overshadowed. Authority is absent. Hedge ratio < 1:1.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Frequent hedging and fillers. Filler rate 5–7 per minute. Speaker
consistently softens positions. Assertive language is occasional
and not the pattern. Hedge ratio approaching 1:1 but not reaching it.

Score 4 — Developing:
More hedging than assertive language overall. Filler rate 4–6 per
minute. Hedging patterns appear regularly. Some assertive phrases
exist but overall impression is tentative. Hedge ratio 1:1 to 1.5:1.

Score 5 — Developing:
Mixed. Fillers and hedging present roughly equally alongside assertive
language. Filler rate 3–5 per minute. Some moments of authority,
undermined by qualification elsewhere. Hedge ratio approximately 1:1
to 2:1.

Score 6 — Developing:
Mostly assertive. Hedges appear occasionally but the overall
impression is one of confidence. Filler rate 2–4 per minute.
Declarative statements dominate. Hedge ratio 2:1 to 3:1.

Score 7 — Strong:
Predominantly assertive. Hedging is rare. Filler rate 1–2 per minute.
The speaker takes clear positions and holds them. Assertive language
projects authority consistently. Hedge ratio 3:1 or higher.

Score 8 — Strong:
Highly assertive throughout. Filler rate 0–2 per minute. Clear,
direct declarations. Any residual hedge is isolated. The overall
impression is one of command. Hedge ratio 4:1 or higher.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional language authority. Filler rate 0–1 per minute.
Speaker states positions with conviction. Word choices project
certainty and ownership throughout. Active use of conclusive
language ("therefore", "the answer is clear", "this means").
Hedge ratio 5:1 or higher.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class language of authority. Filler rate 0 across the
session. Every sentence conveys mastery and confidence. A model
of executive communication. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Always state both the filler rate and the assertive/hedge pattern.
Scores 1–3: "[X] filler words per minute were detected in this session.
  [specific hedge phrases heard] appeared multiple times. When language
  is full of qualifiers and filler sounds, your audience hears doubt —
  even when your ideas are strong. The hedging tells them you are not
  fully committed to what you are saying."
Scores 4–6: "Your filler rate of [X] per minute and use of phrases
  like [example] are creating a pattern of qualified communication.
  Every time you soften a statement, you invite your audience to
  soften their response to it."
Scores 7–8: "Your language is predominantly assertive and your filler
  rate of [X] per minute is well-controlled. The next level is replacing
  any remaining qualifiers with declarative statements — 'I think this
  is the right approach' becomes 'this is the right approach.'"
Scores 9–10: Name the specific assertive language patterns that
  earned the score.

FILLER WORD DIAGNOSTIC (always included separately):
  Report: total filler count, filler rate per minute, breakdown by
  type (e.g., "um: 4, uh: 2, like: 6, you know: 1"). Frame as data,
  not judgment. "These are the filler words detected in your session.
  Reducing these is one of the fastest ways to increase how authoritative
  you sound."

DIMENSION 10 — STRUCTURE
Pillar: Thought Clarity | Weight: 15% (audio-only: 18%)
Source: gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether the response has clear, confident architecture — an opening that
signals where the communication is going, an organised body where ideas
build logically, and a decisive close. The highest standard is point-first
delivery: stating the conclusion or recommendation before the reasoning.
This is the single most cited differentiator of senior-level thinking in
executive communication research. Scored from the transcript — this
dimension assesses what was said and in what order.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
  - Opening frame: does a clear purpose or conclusion appear within
    the first 15 seconds of speech?
  - Point-first delivery: is the recommendation or main point stated
    before the supporting rationale?
  - Logical sequencing: do ideas follow in cause-and-effect, priority,
    or chronological order that a listener could outline?
  - Transition density: count of explicit connective phrases per minute
    ("because of that", "which means", "as a result", "building on that",
    "on the other hand", "this leads to", "therefore")
  - Closing frame: does an explicit conclusion, recommendation, or
    completion signal appear at the end?

QUANTITATIVE REFERENCE:

  Transition density (per minute of speech):
    Needs Work:    < 0.5 transitions per minute
    Developing:    0.5–1.5 transitions per minute
    Strong:        1.5–3.0 transitions per minute
    Distinguished: 3.0+ transitions per minute with variety of types

  Opening frame timing:
    Distinguished: Within first 10 seconds
    Strong:        Within first 15 seconds
    Developing:    Present but after 15 seconds
    Needs Work:    Absent

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
No discernible structure. Speaker jumps between topics without
connection. No opening frame. No close. The response does not
address the prompt. The listener cannot extract a main point.
< 0.3 transitions per minute.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Severely disorganized. One or two points appear but are not
connected or sequenced. Prompt barely addressed. No functional
opening or close. < 0.5 transitions per minute.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Minimal structure. A vague beginning exists but the body wanders
with little logic. The close is absent or trails off. Prompt
partially addressed. < 0.7 transitions per minute.

Score 4 — Developing:
Some structure visible but inconsistent. A functional opening may
exist without a clear body plan, or the body is organised but the
close is weak. Speaker starts well but loses the thread. 0.5–1.0
transitions per minute.

Score 5 — Developing:
Moderate structure. A recognisable opening, a somewhat organised
middle, and an attempt at a close. Significant gaps or wanders
remain. Prompt addressed but not sharply. 0.8–1.3 transitions
per minute. No point-first delivery.

Score 6 — Developing:
Solid structure. Clear opening that addresses the prompt. Organised
body with a logical flow. A close that brings it together. Minor
gaps only. 1.2–1.8 transitions per minute. Point-first delivery
attempted but not consistently achieved.

Score 7 — Strong:
Well-structured throughout. Opening within 15 seconds. Body flows
without redundancy. Close is decisive. 1.5–2.5 transitions per
minute with logical variety. Point-first delivery present in at
least part of the response.

Score 8 — Strong:
Strong, intentional structure. Opening hooks within 10–15 seconds.
Body is without redundancy. Close is decisive and links to the
opening. 2.0–3.0 transitions per minute. Point-first delivery
consistently applied. The response has been clearly planned.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional structure. Thesis stated in the opening sentence.
Body is a clear argument that a listener could outline on first
hearing. Close links explicitly back to the opening thesis.
3.0+ transitions per minute with genuine variety of connective
types. Point-first delivery throughout.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
Masterful architecture. The structure itself communicates authority.
Opening, body, and close form a seamless, persuasive whole. A
listener could immediately repeat the structure of the argument.
All criteria for 9 at the highest level throughout. Exceptionally
rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Always reference the specific structural elements observed or missing.
Scores 1–3: "Your response did not have a clear opening, organised
  body, or definitive close. When communication has no structure,
  your audience has to do the organisational work themselves. At
  senior levels, that work should be done before you speak, not
  by the people listening to you."
Scores 4–6: Name specifically what is present and what is missing.
  "You opened your response with [observation] but the body moved
  between [topics] without signposting the connections. Your audience
  could follow each individual point but could not see how they
  built into an argument."
Scores 7–8: "Your structure is working well — you opened clearly,
  organised your points logically, and closed decisively. The next
  level is leading with your recommendation in the very first sentence,
  before any context or rationale. This is point-first delivery — the
  way the most senior communicators operate."
Scores 9–10: Name the specific structural choices that earned the
  score and their impact.

DIMENSION 11 — CONCISENESS
Pillar: Thought Clarity | Weight: 7% (audio-only: 8%)
Source: gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether the speaker says what needs to be said and stops. Over-explanation,
repetition, and padding are among the most common executive presence
detractors in intelligent professionals — particularly those who feel
compelled to justify every point, caveat every statement, or fill silence
with additional words. Conciseness is not brevity for its own sake. It is
the discipline of trusting that a well-made point has landed and that
adding more words dilutes rather than strengthens it.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - gpt-4o-mini-transcribe transcript
  - Total word count relative to speech duration and context category
  - Repetition detection: instances where the same point is restated
    without adding new information
  - Padding detection: phrases that add length without meaning
    ("as I said", "what I mean to say is", "basically what I'm
    trying to say", "if that makes sense", "you know what I mean",
    re-stating conclusions already made)
  - Context-expected duration: the appropriate length for the prompt
    type and context category
  - Over-run flag: if session exceeds context-appropriate length by
    more than 40%, flag as a conciseness concern

CONTEXT-APPROPRIATE LENGTH REFERENCE:
  Category 1 — High Energy / Vision: 60–120 seconds optimal
  Category 2 — Formal Presentation: 90–180 seconds optimal
  Category 3 — Analytical / Technical: 90–180 seconds optimal
  Category 4 — Difficult Conversation: 60–120 seconds optimal
  Category 5 — Conversational / Interview: 60–90 seconds optimal

  These are benchmarks, not hard limits. Longer responses are not
  automatically penalised — repetition and padding are. A 3-minute
  response that adds value throughout scores better than a 90-second
  response that repeats its opening point three times.

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Response is severely bloated. The same points are restated multiple
times without adding information. Extensive padding throughout.
Significantly over the context-appropriate length. A listener would
struggle to identify what the speaker's main point was within the
volume of words.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Heavy repetition of key points. Multiple padding phrases throughout.
Significant over-run on expected duration. The response would be
materially stronger at half its length without losing any content.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Frequent repetition and consistent over-explanation. The speaker
revisits points already made without adding value. Noticeable
padding. Response exceeds context-appropriate length meaningfully.

Score 4 — Developing:
Some repetition and over-explanation — particularly at the end of
the response where the speaker restates conclusions already clearly
made. Occasional padding phrases. Slightly over context-appropriate
length.

Score 5 — Developing:
Moderate efficiency. Most points made once but with occasional
re-statements or qualifiers that add length without clarity.
Some padding. Generally within acceptable length but with room
to trim meaningfully.

Score 6 — Developing:
Mostly concise with isolated instances of repetition or padding.
Within context-appropriate length. The response is coherent and
efficient except for one or two passages that could be removed
without loss.

Score 7 — Strong:
Concise throughout. Each point made once. No meaningful repetition.
Minimal padding. Within context-appropriate length. The response
respects the listener's time at every moment.

Score 8 — Strong:
Noticeably efficient. Every sentence adds value. No repetition.
No padding detected. The speaker trusts their points to land and
moves on. Response length is appropriate to the content without
over- or under-serving the prompt.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional conciseness. Not a word is wasted. Every sentence
either introduces new information, advances the argument, or
closes a section. The response would not be improved by any
addition or removal. The listener receives precisely what they
need and nothing more.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
All criteria for 9 at the highest level. The economy of language
is itself a presence signal — the speaker's discipline in saying
exactly what is needed and no more communicates mastery.
Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: "Your response restated [specific point] [number] times
  and included phrases like [example padding phrase] that added length
  without adding meaning. At senior levels, over-explanation signals
  doubt — as if you do not trust your point to have landed. Saying
  something once, clearly, is more powerful than saying it three times
  with qualifiers."
Scores 4–6: Name the specific repetition or padding observed.
  "The clearest opportunity is [specific passage] — this point was
  already made at [earlier point in response]. Removing the restatement
  would make the response sharper without losing anything."
Scores 7–8: "Your response is concise and efficient. The next level
  is identifying the one sentence in your response that adds the least
  — and removing it. The discipline of cutting what is 'fine' to keep
  only what is 'necessary' is what distinguishes Strong from
  Distinguished."
Scores 9–10: Confirm the economy and its impact.
  "Every sentence in this response earned its place. Your audience
  received exactly what they needed and nothing more — that is the
  highest form of respect for the listener's attention."

DIMENSION 12 — EYE CONTACT
Pillar: Physical Delivery | Weight: 8% | Source: Claude Vision video frames
Video sessions only. Not scored in audio-only mode.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
How consistently and intentionally the speaker connects with the camera —
the equivalent of direct eye contact in a room. Strong eye contact creates
the feeling of being spoken to personally. Avoiding the camera signals
discomfort or disengagement, even when the words are confident. Scored
from video frame analysis only.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Claude Vision eyeContactObservation field (qualitative assessment
    of gaze direction and consistency across captured frames)
  - Frame-by-frame gaze direction: on-camera, off to side, downward,
    upward, closed
  - faceLostEvents count (how many times the face left frame entirely)
  - faceCoverageFlag (triggered if face lost more than 3 times)
  - Frames are captured every 8 seconds (first frame at 2.5 seconds),
    up to 10 frames per session. All visual scoring based on these frames.

FRAME SCORING GUIDE:
  For each frame, classify gaze as one of:
    DIRECT: Speaker looking at or very near the camera lens
    NEAR: Speaker looking close to camera — slight deviation
    OFF: Speaker looking clearly away — to side, down, or up
    ABSENT: Face not in frame

  Calculate: direct + near frames as percentage of total scoreable frames.

GAZE SCORE REFERENCE:
  Needs Work:    Direct/near in < 40% of frames
  Developing:    Direct/near in 40–65% of frames
  Strong:        Direct/near in 66–80% of frames
  Distinguished: Direct/near in > 80% of frames, with warm and
                 deliberate quality assessed by Claude Vision

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Speaker never looking at or near the camera across frames. Eyes
consistently cast down, sideways, or away. The viewer experiences
no personal connection at any point.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Camera connection nearly absent. Rare glances toward camera in 1
of 10 frames or fewer. No sustained eye contact across the session.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Occasional camera connection in 2–3 frames. Speaker frequently
looks away. Consistency entirely absent. Direct/near frames < 30%.

Score 4 — Developing:
Some camera connection but unreliable. Direct/near in 35–45% of
frames. Several frames show good gaze; others show speaker looking
away. The viewer would notice the inconsistency.

Score 5 — Developing:
Moderate eye contact. Speaker connects with camera more often than
not but breaks are frequent enough to weaken the effect. Direct/near
in 45–60% of frames.

Score 6 — Developing:
Good eye contact in most frames. Direct/near in 60–70% of frames.
Breaks are infrequent. Viewer feels connected most of the time
but not consistently.

Score 7 — Strong:
Strong eye contact. Direct/near in 70–80% of frames. Breaks are
rare and brief. The viewer feels reliably spoken to throughout.

Score 8 — Strong:
Excellent eye contact. Consistent, warm, direct camera connection
in > 78% of frames. The viewer feels personally engaged.
Claude Vision assesses quality of connection as warm and genuine.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Exceptional. Direct/near in > 83% of frames. Gaze is deliberate
and commanding. Connection sustained and purposeful across the
entire session. Claude Vision assesses the eye contact as
intentional and confident throughout.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class eye contact. Direct/near in > 88% of frames. Speaker's
gaze communicates full presence and authority. A benchmark for
video delivery. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Always reference the frame-level observation pattern.
Scores 1–3: "In most of the frames captured from your session, you
  were not looking at the camera. To the person watching, this reads
  as disengagement — as if you are speaking at the room rather than
  to them. Eye contact through the camera is the virtual equivalent
  of looking someone in the eye. Without it, your presence is
  significantly diminished."
Scores 4–6: "Your camera connection was inconsistent — you connected
  well in some moments and looked away in others. The breaks in
  contact are where your audience momentarily loses the sense of
  being spoken to directly. Consistent camera connection is one of
  the fastest-return improvements available in video communication."
Scores 7–8: Confirm and identify the next level.
Scores 9–10: Name the quality of connection observed.

DIMENSION 13 — FACIAL EXPRESSION
Pillar: Physical Delivery | Weight: 4% | Source: Claude Vision video frames
Video sessions only. Not scored in audio-only mode.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether the speaker's facial expression matches what they are saying and
conveys appropriate warmth, conviction, and engagement. A face that is
flat when the content is important, or animated at the wrong moments,
creates incongruence that the audience registers instinctively — even
if they cannot name what feels off. Scored from video frame analysis.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Claude Vision presenceObservation and gestureObservation fields
    for facial animation assessment
  - Frame-by-frame assessment of: expression congruence with content,
    baseline warmth vs tension, expression range across the session,
    presence of anxiety signals in the face (tight jaw, pressed lips,
    furrowed brow at baseline)
  - faceLostEvents count

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Expression is completely flat throughout all frames, or actively
incongruent — smiling when delivering serious content, or showing
visible distress. The face contradicts the message in a way the
viewer cannot ignore.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Expression is either absent (frozen or blank across nearly all frames)
or significantly mismatched to content in multiple frames. Very little
warmth or engagement visible. Anxiety signals dominant in the face.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Minimal expression that does not track the content. Occasional
movement but no clear relationship between what is being said and
what the face is doing. The viewer receives no emotional signal.

Score 4 — Developing:
Some expression present but inconsistent. Moments of appropriate
engagement visible in some frames, absent in others. Expression
range is limited. Occasional mismatch with content.

Score 5 — Developing:
Expression is generally appropriate but not expressive. A
predominantly neutral face that occasionally animates. The viewer
receives a sense of engagement but not genuine connection.

Score 6 — Developing:
Mostly appropriate expression with some warmth visible. Minor
incongruences in 1–2 frames. Expression range present but limited.
The viewer generally feels the speaker is present and engaged.

Score 7 — Strong:
Expression consistently matches content across frames. Warm baseline.
Visible range — the face conveys gravity on serious points and
warmth on human moments. Minor lapses only.

Score 8 — Strong:
Expressive and congruent throughout. The face actively reinforces
the message. Warmth is genuine and visible. Expression range is
clear and appropriate to the content. Claude Vision assesses the
expression as engaged and natural.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Expression is a communication tool. The viewer watching without
audio could identify what the speaker considers important, serious,
or positive from the face alone. Congruence is consistent. Warmth
is genuine. No anxiety signals visible.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class expressiveness and congruence. The face and voice are
a unified instrument. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: "Your face was largely [flat / incongruent] across the
  session. Your audience processes your face and your words
  simultaneously — when they don't match, the face wins. A flat
  or mismatched expression creates doubt even when the words are
  confident."
Scores 4–6: Name the specific gap — flat baseline, limited range,
  or occasional mismatch. "Your expression tells your audience how
  you feel about what you are saying. When it is neutral throughout,
  they have no signal that any point matters more than any other."
Scores 7–10: Confirm what is working and what the next level looks like.

DIMENSION 14 — GESTURES
Pillar: Physical Delivery | Weight: 3% | Source: Claude Vision video frames
Video sessions only. Not scored in audio-only mode.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
Whether hand and arm movements serve the communication or compete with
it. Purposeful gestures — those that emphasise a point, enumerate ideas,
or illustrate a relationship — amplify the message. Nervous or repetitive
movements draw attention away from it. The standard is not frequency of
gesture but intentionality. Scored from video frame analysis.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Claude Vision gestureObservation field
  - Frame-by-frame assessment of: purposeful gesture presence,
    nervous or repetitive movement (fidgeting, self-touching),
    open vs closed body position (arms crossed, hands gripped),
    gesture frame (are movements within a controlled area or erratic)

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Gestures are actively harmful — erratic, distracting, or nervous
movements that undermine presence across frames. Hands create
negative attention throughout.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Movements are mostly distracting. Persistent fidgeting, self-touching,
or constant movement visible in multiple frames. Closed body
throughout.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Either completely rigid with no expressive movement at all, or
gesture patterns consistently distract from the message. No
purposeful gestures visible across frames.

Score 4 — Developing:
Gestures present but stiff or mechanical. Some purposeful movement
but it lacks naturalness. Occasional closed body position.

Score 5 — Developing:
Some purposeful gesturing with some awkward or absent moments.
Movement is uneven across the session. Open body generally but
with lapses.

Score 6 — Developing:
Good use of gesture in most frames. Movements generally support
and reinforce content. Open body. Minor stiffness or missed
opportunities only.

Score 7 — Strong:
Natural, purposeful gesturing visible across frames. Hands work
with the message. Movements feel unscripted and appropriate.
Open body throughout.

Score 8 — Strong:
Excellent gesture work. Hands are an active communication tool.
Movements expressive, appropriately timed, and reinforcing key
points. Open, confident body position throughout.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Gesture and movement fully integrated with speech. Physical
delivery amplifies the message at key moments. Natural rather
than performed — gestures emerge from the content.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class physical expressiveness. Gesture and movement seamless
and powerful throughout. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: Name the specific movement pattern observed.
  "Nervous movements pull your audience's attention to your hands
  rather than your message. Every moment they are watching your
  hands is a moment they are not receiving what you are saying."
Scores 4–6: "Your gestures are present but not yet working as a
  tool. Purposeful movement — a hand that counts out three points,
  or opens to indicate scale — reinforces your words in a way that
  makes them more memorable."
Scores 7–10: Confirm and build. Higher scores: "Your gestures are
  a genuine asset — they make your communication more vivid without
  drawing attention to themselves."

DIMENSION 15 — POSTURE
Pillar: Physical Delivery | Weight: 5% | Source: Claude Vision video frames
Video sessions only. Not scored in audio-only mode.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IS MEASURED:
How the speaker holds and carries themselves physically — spinal
alignment, shoulder position, stillness, and whether the body signals
settled authority or managed anxiety. Posture communicates the speaker's
relationship to the conversation before a word is spoken. Scored from
video frame analysis.

INPUT SIGNALS:
  - Claude Vision presenceObservation field
  - Frame-by-frame assessment of: spinal alignment (upright vs
    slumped), shoulder position (level and open vs raised or
    collapsed), head position (aligned vs forward-jutting or
    tilted), visible tension (gripped hands, tense shoulders,
    physical contraction), deliberate forward lean at key moments

SCORE ANCHORS:

Score 1 — Needs Work:
Posture is collapsed or severely slumped throughout frames.
Physical presentation actively signals anxiety or disengagement.
The body contradicts any verbal authority.

Score 2 — Needs Work:
Significantly poor posture in most frames. Either slumped, hunched,
or physically contracted in a way that communicates low confidence
throughout.

Score 3 — Needs Work:
Posture is noticeably poor — below professional standard in most
frames. Inconsistent head position or visible physical tension.
The body is working against the speaker.

Score 4 — Developing:
Posture generally attempts upright but with clear lapses. Shoulders
occasionally collapsed or raised. Tension visible in some frames.
The physical signal is mixed.

Score 5 — Developing:
Generally upright but with noticeable inconsistency. Some frames
show strong posture; others show slouching or physical contraction.
The signal is not reliable.

Score 6 — Developing:
Mostly upright and open. Minor lapses in a few frames. Generally
professional standard with room to improve consistency.

Score 7 — Strong:
Upright, open posture consistently across frames. Head aligned.
Shoulders level and open. Physical presentation supports authority.
Only very minor lapses.

Score 8 — Strong:
Clearly strong, settled posture throughout. The body communicates
composure and confidence. No meaningful lapses. Deliberate
forward lean visible in at least one frame at an appropriate moment.

Score 9 — Distinguished:
Posture is a presence signal in its own right. Consistently upright,
open, and settled. Deliberate postural variation visible —
leaning forward at key moments, grounded stillness in others.
The body is fully in service of the message.

Score 10 — Distinguished:
World-class physical command. The speaker's body communicates
authority before a word is spoken. Every frame shows deliberate,
powerful physical presence. Exceptionally rare.

COACHING FEEDBACK:
Scores 1–3: "Your posture in this session was [description from frames].
  Before your voice makes an impression, your body already has.
  A collapsed or tense posture tells your audience you are not
  settled — and unsettled leaders do not command rooms."
Scores 4–6: "Your posture was inconsistent — some moments were
  strong, others showed [specific observation]. Consistency matters
  because your audience is watching you throughout, not just when
  you are making your key point."
Scores 7–10: Confirm and build. "A settled, upright physical presence
  is one of the most immediate authority signals available to you.
  You are already using it well — the next level is making it the
  foundation everything else builds on."

    Intonation                      8%

  PILLAR 3: THOUGHT CLARITY        Total: 35%
    Confidence Language            13%

  PILLAR 4: PHYSICAL DELIVERY      Total: 20%
    Eye Contact                     8%
    Facial Expression               4%
    Gestures                        3%
    Posture                         5%

  TOTAL                           100%

AUDIO-ONLY SESSION WEIGHTS (11 dimensions, Physical Delivery absent):
  Physical Delivery weight (20%) redistributed proportionally.

  PILLAR 1: VOICE QUALITY          Total: 25%
    Articulation                    5%
    Projection                      7%
    Vocal Tone                      9%
    Vocal Steadiness                4%  (note: Jitter unavailable —
                                        scored from F0 SD and gpt-audio
                                        tremor assessment only)

  PILLAR 2: VOCAL DELIVERY         Total: 30%
    Intonation                     10%
    Pace                            8%
    Pausing                         7%
    Breath Control                  5%

  PILLAR 3: THOUGHT CLARITY        Total: 45%  (note: increased from 35%
    Confidence Language            16%   because Thought Clarity carries
    Structure                      20%   the highest perceptual weight
    Conciseness                     9%   when visual channel is absent)

  TOTAL                           100%

RESEARCH BASIS FOR WEIGHTS:
  Thought Clarity at 35% (video) / 45% (audio): Supported by Sylvia Ann
  Hewlett's research identifying gravitas as accounting for approximately
  60% of executive presence perception. Structure and Confidence Language
  are the most direct measurable proxies for gravitas.

  Vocal Delivery at 25% (video) / 30% (audio): Supported by communication
  science research on paralinguistic signals as dominant credibility
  channels when content and delivery diverge.

  Voice Quality at 20% (video) / 25% (audio): Supported by Klofstad,
  Anderson, and Tigue (2012) on vocal resonance and tone as predictors
  of perceived leadership competence.

  Physical Delivery at 20% (video): Supported by Cuddy's research on
  postural dominance, Ekman's work on facial expression and trust, and
  eye contact research in virtual communication contexts.

6.2 COMPOSITE CALCULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Step 1: Multiply each dimension score (integer 1–10) by its weight.
Step 2: Sum all weighted scores.
Step 3: Apply gating rules (Section 6.3).
Step 4: Round to one decimal place for display.
Step 5: Assign tier from the composite score.

COMPOSITE = sum of (dimension score × dimension weight) for all
            applicable dimensions, subject to gating rules.

6.3 GATING RULES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ANCHOR DIMENSIONS:
  Video mode:     Structure, Vocal Tone, Intonation, Eye Contact
  Audio-only mode: Structure, Vocal Tone, Intonation

These represent the highest-weighted dimension from each pillar and
are the foundational signals of executive presence. A speaker cannot
be rated Strong or Distinguished overall if any anchor dimension is
in the Needs Work band.

RULE 1 — NEEDS WORK CAP:
  If any anchor dimension scores 1–3 (Needs Work), the composite is
  capped at 6.4 regardless of how well other dimensions scored.
  Rationale: a speaker cannot be rated Strong overall if a foundational
  dimension is severely deficient, even if individual delivery mechanics
  are polished elsewhere.

  Application: Calculate the weighted composite normally. If the result
  would be above 6.4 but an anchor dimension is 1–3, set composite to
  6.4 and display a note: "Your composite score has been adjusted because
  [Dimension Name] is in the Needs Work range. Strengthening this
  foundational dimension is the highest-return action available to you."

RULE 2 — DISTINGUISHED GATE:
  To receive a composite of 8.5 or above (Distinguished tier), the
  speaker must meet both of the following conditions:
    Condition A: All anchor dimensions score 7 or above.
    Condition B: At least 10 of 15 dimensions score 7 or above
                 (video mode), OR at least 8 of 11 dimensions score 7
                 or above (audio-only mode).

  If the weighted composite calculates to 8.5 or above but either
  condition fails, the composite is capped at 8.4 (Strong tier).
  Display note: "Your composite score has been adjusted to Strong. To
  reach Distinguished, all foundational dimensions must score Strong
  or above, and consistent strength is required across the majority
  of dimensions."

RULE 3 — AUDIO-ONLY REBALANCING:
  When video is unavailable (face coverage < 80%), Physical Delivery
  dimensions are removed and their combined weight (20%) is redistributed
  proportionally across the remaining three pillars using the audio-only
  weights in Section 6.1. The audio-only anchor dimensions apply.
  Notify user that Physical Delivery dimensions were not scored.

6.4 TIER ASSIGNMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Composite 1.0–3.9   →  Needs Work
  Composite 4.0–6.4   →  Developing
  Composite 6.5–8.4   →  Strong
  Composite 8.5–10.0  →  Distinguished (subject to Gate in Rule 2)

  Note: A composite of 8.9 that fails the Distinguished Gate is assigned
  8.4 (Strong). The gate takes precedence over the numeric threshold.
  Display to user as the capped value (8.4), not the pre-cap value.

6.5 COMPOSITE DISPLAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Dimension scores: displayed as integers (e.g. 7)
  Composite score: displayed to one decimal place (e.g. 7.4)
  Tier: displayed alongside composite (e.g. 7.4 — Strong)
  Gating adjustments: always disclosed to the user with explanation

SECTION 7 — SCORING CONSISTENCY REQUIREMENTS
================================================================================

7.1 CALIBRATION STANDARD
  Any scorer — human or AI — applying this document to the same recording
  must arrive within ±0.5 points per dimension of the reference score.
  Composite scores must agree within ±0.3. Larger discrepancies indicate
  an anchor or decision rule is insufficiently specific and must be
  flagged for methodology review.

7.2 SCORE 9 AND 10 RARITY
  Scores of 9 and 10 are rare. A 9 means the scoring engine found almost
  nothing meaningful to improve in that dimension. A 10 is reserved for
  world-class delivery rarely encountered even among professional speakers.
  If the scoring engine awards 9 or 10, the coaching feedback must
  specifically name what earned it — a score of 9 or 10 with generic
  feedback is a scoring error.

7.3 SCORE 7 STANDARD
  A 7 is a genuinely good score. Do not inflate to 8 or 9 to be
  encouraging. A 7 means: consistent, controlled, credible, solid
  professional standard with only minor gaps. The coaching feedback
  for a 7 should acknowledge real strength while identifying the
  specific gap that keeps it from 8.

7.4 CROSS-DIMENSION CONFLICT RESOLUTION
  F0 SD is used in both Intonation (Dimension 5) and Vocal Steadiness
  (Dimension 4). When F0 SD is high:
    - If gpt-audio assesses the variation as expressive and purposeful:
      scores well in Intonation, neutral in Vocal Steadiness.
    - If gpt-audio assesses the variation as tremor or anxiety-driven:
      scores poorly in Vocal Steadiness, neutral in Intonation.
  These are mutually exclusive interpretations. The gpt-audio qualitative
  assessment is the deciding signal when the numeric value is ambiguous.

7.5 TRANSCRIPT DEPENDENCY ACKNOWLEDGEMENT
  Structure and Conciseness are scored from gpt-4o-mini-transcribe output.
  This model produces clean, readable transcripts adequate for semantic
  structure assessment. It may normalise some speech patterns. If the
  transcript appears to have missed significant content, note this in
  the output and reduce scoring confidence for these two dimensions.

SECTION 8 — FILLER WORD DIAGNOSTIC REPORT
================================================================================

Filler words are reported as a standalone diagnostic in addition to
their role as a sub-signal of Confidence Language (Dimension 9).
This report appears in the results for every session and is separate
from the Confidence Language score.

REPORT CONTENTS:
  - Total filler word count (from gpt-audio)
  - Filler rate: total count divided by speech duration in minutes
  - Breakdown by type: um, uh, ah, like, you know, so, basically,
    literally, right, okay, I mean, kind of, sort of
  - Note: "This count is based on what was audible in your recording.
    Soft or quiet filler sounds may occasionally not be detected."

FRAMING IN FEEDBACK:
  Present as data, not judgment.
  "These are the filler words detected in your session:
  [breakdown by type and count]
  Your filler rate was [X] per minute. For reference, [Strong /
  Distinguished] communicators typically use fewer than [2 / 1]
  per minute. Reducing filler words is one of the fastest ways to
  increase how authoritative you sound — the silence that replaces
  a filler word is always more powerful than the filler itself."
